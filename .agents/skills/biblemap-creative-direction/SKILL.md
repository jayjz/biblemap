# BibleMap Creative Direction

Design passage-led experiences that preserve Scripture, historical integrity, and visitor agency.

Compare distinct creative directions in proportion to the task; do not let concept studies delay a coherent, implemented visitor journey. Evaluate what visitors encounter, discover, question, and remember.

Use original illustration, typography, visual metaphor, licensed photography, historical maps, animation, sound, and archival art according to their narrative purpose.

Never confuse artistic invention with historical evidence.

Prefer meaningful discovery over decorative spectacle. Preserve theological ambiguity and editorial authority.

## Narrative integrity before visual execution

For a human-story journey, first establish a concise, source-supported narrative spine. An unfamiliar visitor should be able to understand:

- Who the people were, as far as surviving evidence allows, and whose voice preserves their stories.
- The world, relationships, obligations, and circumstances that shaped the events.
- What happened and why one moment leads to the next, without filling historical silences with invented motives.
- Which details are testimony, historical evidence, theological interpretation, later tradition, or artistic reconstruction.
- What remains unresolved and why it matters.

Write a cohesive introduction and connective prose before splitting the account into visual movements. Each scene should change what the visitor knows, notices, or questions; a source containing another event is not sufficient reason for another scene. Preserve continuity, transitions, and readable language across the whole journey, including mobile.

Art, history, theology, philosophy, and culture should illuminate the people and their context rather than become separate explanatory panels. Allow primary testimony to speak where it is stronger than interpretation. AI-authored summaries and transitions must be sourced, labeled as editorial drafts, and reviewed by the human creator; do not imitate an unverified personal voice.

An ending should grow from the story and the limits of the surviving record. Never attach a generic reflection question to compensate for weak narrative development. Do not prescribe the reader's emotion, belief, or verdict.

Test the narrative separately from its visual polish: can an unfamiliar reader explain who these people were, what happened, whose account they encountered, and where certainty ends? Technical checks and author intent cannot demonstrate that outcome without reader observation.

## Medium-neutral creative exploration

Before settling on an archival-art composition, consider whether another artistic medium could communicate the passage more powerfully.

Explore genuinely different directions before choosing; use the brief to set the scope of comparison. No medium, palette, typography, layout or transition is prescribed.

Potential approaches include:

- Original symbolic or illustrative composition
- Literary and typographic storytelling
- Interactive geographical or historical exploration
- Animation revealing chronology or narrative contrast
- Documentary or archival juxtaposition
- Sound, silence, and spatial pacing

These are possibilities, not quotas.

For each proposed direction identify:

1. The biblical or human tension.
2. The artistic mechanism.
3. The intended visitor discovery.
4. Historical and theological representation risks.
5. Rights, provenance, and editorial requirements.

Do not confuse originality with novelty for its own sake.

A creative concept succeeds when its artistic choices illuminate the material without fabricating evidence or prescribing interpretation.
