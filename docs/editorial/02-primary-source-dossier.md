# Perpetua: consequential source dossier

Checked 1 November 2026. Research draft for [treatment v2](04-narrative-treatment-v2.md). The question is how the account makes care, confession and separation coexist, and how a reader can hear its different speaking positions without being promised an authenticated diary. A passage's presence in a text and the independent historicity of its events are separate judgments.

| --- | --- | --- |
| Prologue §1; frame §2 | The framing voice wants new accounts to strengthen the Church and glorify God. It introduces a group, identifies Perpetua's education, marriage, approximate age and nursing son, and attributes the following writing to her hand and understanding. Felicity is Revocatus's fellow servant; her enslaved status belongs to the frame, not a recovered biography. | State the work's Christian purpose and attribution once. Keep social differences and the group visible; do not call both women interchangeable examples of motherhood. |
| §3 | A name is contested before imprisonment: she identifies as Christian through the vessel comparison. Her father reacts violently; she reports comfort during his absence. Baptism precedes the prison's fear, crowding and nursing difficulty. The deacons' paid intervention, mother and brother, and permission for the child to stay change her situation. The palace comparison follows relief. | Do not begin with a fictional hesitant believer, saintly serenity or a uniformly tender father. Fear and confession already coexist in the text. Recover the sequence of care rather than describing an abstract bond. |
| §4 | Her brother asks whether the outcome will be suffering or escape. She reports a ladder, serpent, shepherd and food; on waking they interpret the vision as pointing toward martyrdom. Saturus appears here within her reported vision before his own attributed account. | Place this change in expectation before the hearing. A vision can be narratively decisive without being presented as externally verified geography. |
| §§5–6 | The father appeals through his age, upbringing, family and child; kisses her hands, falls at her feet and calls her Lady. She narrates grief for him and confidence in God's power. At the hearing, family appeal becomes an official demand for sacrifice; she refuses, confesses Christian identity, sees her father struck and is condemned. | Let their represented words conflict on the page. A father who pleads and a daughter who refuses need no invented interior monologues. His statement that the child cannot live without her is an appeal, not a documented infant prognosis. |
| End §6 | After a cheerful return following condemnation, the account immediately requests the nursing child through Pomponius. The father refuses. The paragraph then assigns the child's no longer desiring the breast and the mother's lack of bodily distress to God's will. | Keep request, refusal and religious explanation together. Do not turn bodily relief into indifference, a medically verified miracle, a reunion, or evidence that the child died. The theological meaning belongs to this attributed narrative. |
| §§7–8; §9 | Dinocrates is remembered in prayer: a dead younger brother, thirst, unreachable water, repeated prayer, then drinking and play in a second vision. The attributed account interprets his changed state. Later Pudens allows visitors; the father returns in grief and she again reports sorrow for him. | Nourishment, denied access and relief recur in different registers. This is a literary observation, not proof of displaced maternal grief. Do not diagnose visions or turn their interpretation into a doctrinal consensus. The father does not simply disappear after the refusal. |
| §10 | A reported contest differs from the expected beasts; the vision includes bodily/gender transformation and an Egyptian opponent. Waking interpretation identifies the struggle as against the devil. Attributed writing explicitly stops before the games. | Retain the strange particulars rather than reduce the vision to generic victory. No literal combat interaction, ethnic caricature, universal gender interpretation or heavenly reconstruction. Compare dream, waking interpretation and narratorial ending without merging them. |
| §§11–13; §14 | The frame attributes a separate written vision to Saturus. His first-person account places Perpetua beside him and represents speech by her within **his** vision; it includes reconciliation involving bishop and presbyter. The narrator resumes in §14. | A quotation containing Perpetua's speech is not automatically her writing. Give Saturus a named speaking position and a genuine excerpt, rather than hiding him in provenance prose. |
| §15 | The narrator represents Felicity's fear of being left behind, the companions' grief and prayer, childbirth, an attendant's taunt, her response about suffering and another's presence, and a daughter reared by a sister. | Her desire to remain with her companions is essential and unsettling. Reported speech is available even without a first-person document. The passage's statement about pregnant women's punishment requires independent legal research before generalization; no medical explanation or exact pregnancy chronology is supplied here. |
| §§16–21 | The narrator claims a duty to complete the account, reports resistant speech and the spectators' curiosity, and frames the arena as victory. §20 includes Perpetua raising Felicity; §21 narrates deaths and a final explanation of Perpetua's agency. | The ending is both physically particular and theologically interpreted. Use the hand offered to another person; state the deaths plainly. Do not make the final narrator's explanation a neutral observation or give Perpetua a posthumous diary entry. |

## Translation decisions that change the scene

1. **Desire is not a medical conclusion.** L1, §6, p. 9 has *neque ille amplius mammas desiderat* and *neque mihi fervorem fecerunt*. P1 renders the former as no longer needing to nurse and the latter as “nor did I take fever.” P2 uses desire and bodily uneasiness. The nursing relationship is explicit; a diagnosis, mechanism and duration are not. The proof uses P2's older wording rather than adding a medical paraphrase.
2. **Read the refusal as a complete sequence.** L1, §6, p. 8 and P2 II.2 include refusal to sacrifice, the official's question and her confession. P1's electronically modernized §6 moves from the official's demand directly to the confession. Do not build a verbatim courtroom exchange by silently combining translations. The treatment's hearing excerpts name P2.
3. **Older translators also interpret.** P2's chapter V heading says childbirth occurs without suffering, while its body describes labour pain. P2's polemical Dinocrates footnotes and L1's Catholic introduction give opposed doctrinal readings. Neither apparatus is the ancient passage. The proposed excerpts exclude these headings/notes and identify editorial omissions.
4. **One translation is not transparent access.** The proof's P2 text retains its dated vocabulary. Its transcription's `no long` is displayed as `no [longer]`, with that correction disclosed. A qualified editor should choose and review a readable final translation before route integration; do not silently modernize a quoted edition.

## The unresolved scholarship that matters

**S1, read in full:** Muehlberger distinguishes a character's speaking voice from the person composing it (pp. 315–318). She challenges circular dating and alleged early reception (pp. 322–327), and proposes a late fourth- or early fifth-century composition connected with commemoration and rhetorical speech in character (pp. 333–340). Her conclusion acknowledges that she cannot identify a composer, precise place or materials (pp. 340–342). This is an argued alternative, not a demonstrated author identification or a consensus date. It prevents this treatment from treating vivid first-person detail as proof of authenticity. The argument should be evaluated against a current critical edition and direct evidence for early reception, not merely against the confident modern introduction at Fordham. [Author-deposited article](https://works.hcommons.org/records/nykrz-3k844).

**Counterpoint and limits:** L1, introduction xviii–xx, argues from differences in Latin style and prose rhythm for distinct authentic accounts and Latin priority. This is an inspected historical scholarly position, not a current settlement; its reasoning also considers Greek. S3 provides the next modern edition for checking those claims, but its apparatus was not available in this run. S2's publicly accessible summary supports considering agency and power; it cannot supply an unread answer to this authorship dispute. Do not invent a balance of expert votes.

Lineage: transmitted Passion → editorial Latin/Greek witnesses and editions → P1/P2/L1. Translation branches are distinct readings of the same work, not independent witnesses to its events. S1/L1/S3 interpret that work; C1 is a separate ancient correspondence; G1 a separate geographical reference; M1 a contextual object caption. No independent source inspected here corroborates the mother's exact request or the father's refusal.

## Rights and stopping boundary

The 1885 Wallis print translation is public domain in the United States; the proof credits translator, collection, date and exact transcription. It uses no New Advent hyperlinks, revisions, layout or imagery. P1's electronic form permits educational/personal use and restricts commercial copying; it is linked, not reproduced at length. No Shewring English translation, modern critical translation, article PDF or museum image is committed. Original ancient Latin does not make a modern translation or photograph freely reusable.

The priority historical check before implementation is a qualified comparison of §§3, 5–6, 10, 15 and 20–21 against Heffernan's Latin/Greek apparatus and a reviewed final translation, including manuscript variation and early reception. The research is sufficient to design a source-reading experiment; it is insufficient to certify personal authorship, a prison reconstruction or publication-ready theology.
