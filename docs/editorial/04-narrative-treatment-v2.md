# Perpetua treatment v2: the child, the confession, the telling

Research and editorial draft, 1 November 2026. This proposes one revised encounter. It does not replace the live route. All headings, transitions and curatorial sentences below are **sample editorial copy by Codex**, not the creator's prose. Block quotations are the historical English translation identified below; their first-person form is a voice attributed within the transmitted work, not authenticated access to a composer.

## Artistic argument

Begin with an act of care that changes the prison for the person describing it. Let the account's faith become more particular and more difficult as the reader continues: the same voice that wants her child with her describes relief after he is withheld. The father remains a person she grieves for while refusing him. Felicity's represented hope is not simply to survive childbirth but to remain with her companions. At the end, another narrator describes one woman helping the other stand.

The encounter should leave those tensions available. It need not resolve them into a modern portrait of maternal feeling, a lesson about bravery or a proof of belief. The creator's supplied reflection gives refusal under pressure a real authorial emphasis; the remaining questions in the [interview](03-creator-voice-and-interview.md) remain open.

Use eight short movements in the work's order, with a substantial hinge at separation. This is more space within one narrative, not more destinations or biographies. The increase from six is justified by restoring changes the earlier compression obscured. It is a hypothesis: if the reader study shows that the expanded movement loses the people, shorten the reading rather than defend the count.

## 1. The child can stay

**Primary:** §3; frame §2. Wallis I.1–2.

> I suckled my child, which was now enfeebled with hunger.

Later in that paragraph:

> and the dungeon became to me as it were a palace, so that I preferred being there to being elsewhere.

**Situation and tension:** A recently baptized mother describes fear in custody and care for a hungry infant. The text also preserves her prior refusal to name herself anything but Christian and her father's violent reaction. Care has practical obstacles; confession has already disrupted family contact.

**Beats:** One brief curatorial introduction identifies the group and traditional Carthage setting; the opening does not explain her psychology. Read the fear and nursing passage, then the deacons' intervention, mother/brother care, permission to keep the child and changed description of the prison. Do not extract palace as generic spiritual uplift.

**Reader's change:** Confinement has not ended, but narrated access to the child changes what it is like. **Interaction:** none beyond reading and an optional edition note. Remove relationship buttons here; the people appear in the paragraph doing things. **Voice boundary:** visible attribution to Perpetua within the Passion, translator and §3; explanatory biography sits outside the quote.

**Open question:** What changes when a place of confinement becomes preferable to elsewhere? **Transition:** the next passage begins with her brother asking about the outcome, not a curator promising tragedy. This movement earns the later comparison of relief in two circumstances.

## 2. Her brother asks what will happen

**Primary:** §4. Wallis I.3.

> whether this is to result in a passion or an escape.

At the vision's conclusion:

> and we understood that it was to be a passion, and we ceased henceforth to have any hope in this world.

**Situation and tension:** Her brother asks a question that the attributed account answers by seeking and interpreting a vision. Read selected ladder, serpent, ascent and received food language between those two excerpts; identify all of it as a reported vision. A concise source synopsis can bridge omitted imagery, visibly marked as a synopsis.

**Reader's change:** Before the hearing, the account already represents a shared expectation of death. The impending trial is not an unknown-outcome thriller. **Interaction:** none required. A link opens the complete §4 if the selected passage prompts further reading; it does not reveal an artificially withheld fate. **Voice boundary:** waking interpretation remains in the attributed account, not a curatorial explanation of what the vision objectively proves.

**Open question:** What does that expectation do to the father's appeal that follows? **Transition:** leave the shared conclusion visible above the father's represented appeal. The contrast carries the reader, without an instruction about what to feel.

## 3. A father pleads; she refuses

**Primary:** §§5–6 through condemnation. Wallis II.1–2; the earlier reaction in §3 remains accessible in context.

From the father's represented appeal:

> If with these hands I have brought you up to this flower of your age

From the attributed account:

> with tears he called me not Daughter, but Lady.

At the hearing, the official's demand is followed by:

> And I replied, ‘I will not do so.’ Hilarianus said, ‘Are you a Christian?’ And I replied, ‘I am a Christian.’

**Situation and tension:** His family appeal and her refusal reach a public tribunal. Use the full selected appeal before the hearing excerpt, including the child, kisses and sorrow. After the refusal, retain her grief when he is struck and the sentence. Do not erase his earlier violence or reduce him to it. Do not claim that the missing independent deposition prevents us from hearing how this account portrays him.

**Reader's change:** Refusal is an action within a relationship, with consequences for both people. **Interaction:** none. Speech belongs in writing, not selectable rival testimony cards. **Voice boundary:** father's and official's words are reported inside the attributed account; label this at the passage level, not before every utterance. No courtroom reconstruction.

**Open question:** How can her narrated sorrow for him coexist with refusing what he asks? **Creator placement:** after the complete hearing passage, a future optional note may contain the [actual supplied reflection](03-creator-voice-and-interview.md#verbatim-interview-response), unchanged. Its intended inference and placement need the creator's judgment. No substitute paragraph is written here.

**Transition, sample curatorial copy:** “After the sentence, the account returns to the child.” This simple turn gives the next movement its reason to exist.

## 4. The child is not returned

**Primary:** end §6, with a reversible comparison to §3. Wallis II.2 and I.2.

> Then, because my child had been used to receive suck from me, and to stay with me in the prison, I send Pomponius the deacon to my father to ask for the infant, but my father would not give it him. And even as God willed it, the child no [longer] desired the breast, nor did my breast cause me uneasiness, lest I should be tormented by care for my babe and by the pain of my breasts at once.

`[longer]` corrects the transcription's `no long`; no other modernization is implied.

**Situation and tension:** The person condemned still sends for the child who stayed with her. The refused request is followed immediately by bodily relief given a religious explanation. Do not withhold that second sentence to manufacture grief, then unlock consolation as a reward.

**Reader's change:** The account's language resists a simple narrative of either abandoned faith or abandoned attachment. The reader can now compare relief when the child stays with relief after he is withheld. **Interaction:** one native disclosure, **Read when her child could stay (§3)**, opens the earlier paragraph in full beside/below this passage, retaining §6 in view. It changes the evidence available for comparison, rather than inventing a choice for Perpetua. It can be closed and reopened; reading does not depend on using it.

**Voice boundary:** both passages share the same attributed speaking position, separately located. One optional source note distinguishes narrated relief, theological interpretation and unverified causation. **Open question:** How does the account hold this request and this explanation together? No answer or modern motive is supplied. After the passage, one plain curatorial sentence acknowledges that this paragraph supplies no later life for the child.

**Transition:** continue with the next prayer in the source, for Dinocrates. Do not announce a universal lesson about letting go. This is the only movement built as an [isolated proof](perpetua-separation-proof.html).

## 5. Another child, in a reported vision

**Primary:** §§7–9. Wallis II.3–4 and III.1.

> And I was grieved that, although that pool held water, still, on account of the height to its brink, he could not drink.

The later vision ends with the boy leaving the water:

> to play joyously, after the manner of children

**Situation and tension:** In prayer, the account remembers her dead seven-year-old brother Dinocrates. A vision shows need and inaccessible water; repeated prayer is followed by a second vision and its interpretation. This is not the nursing infant returning under another name. Follow with a short, separately located coda from §9: her father returns in grief, and she again describes sorrow for him.

**Reader's change:** Separation did not end the account's attention to family. Nourishment and access recur in prayer and visions, while the living father remains unresolved. **Interaction:** none. Two plainly numbered passages give first and later vision; no drag-to-lower-water game. **Voice boundary:** reported vision and attributed interpretation remain together; do not import either edition's later doctrinal footnotes.

**Open question:** What relation does the reader see between care, prayer and access across these passages? **Transition:** the approaching games bring another vision, then the boundary of the attributed writing. This short movement earns its place by preventing a secularized account that treats the visions as dispensable ornament. If too long in testing, shorten the excerpts, not their religious register.

## 6. One “I” ends; another begins

**Primary:** §10 and §§11–13. Wallis III.2, IV.1–3.

In the contest vision:

> and I was stripped, and became a man.

At the end of the account:

> but what passed at the exhibition itself let who will write.

Under a new heading, **Saturus's attributed vision**, read:

> ‘Perpetua’ (for she was at my side), ‘this is what the Lord promised to us; we have received the promise.’

**Situation and tension:** The account's final vision represents victory and interprets it against the devil, then gives up the telling. Saturus's attributed voice enters with Perpetua beside him in his vision. The story is not yet the narrator's arena ending.

**Reader's change:** A reader can encounter Perpetua as a speaking “I” and then as someone represented in another “I”'s vision. **Interaction:** a simple optional **Who speaks in this passage?** note identifies the two attributions and the location of her represented reply in §12; no quiz, score or assumed error. **Voice boundary:** a new heading and typographic break accompany actual new text. The excerpts do not use colour alone to differentiate speakers. A complete §10 link preserves the gender imagery, hostile description of the Egyptian and full contest; no simplistic psychological, ethnic or gender conclusion is assigned to them.

**Open question:** What changes in the reader's access when Perpetua is addressed inside Saturus's account? **Transition, sample curatorial copy:** “After these attributed accounts, the narrator resumes.” Do not claim to identify that narrator, their gender or a securely known composition date.

## 7. Felicity does not want to be left behind

**Primary:** frame §2; narrator §§14–15. Wallis I.1, V.1–2.

Of the companions:

> lest they should leave so excellent a friend, and as it were companion, alone in the path of the same hope.

The narrator represents Felicity answering the attendant:

> Now it is I that suffer what I suffer; but then there will be another in me, who will suffer for me, because I also am about to suffer for Him.

**Situation and tension:** Felicity is represented as fearing delayed execution apart from her companions. Their prayer, her childbirth, the attendant's challenge and her answer belong in the reading. The narrator also says another woman rears the daughter. Do not call this a first-person Felicity diary; equally, do not treat the lack of such a diary as a reason to omit her represented speech.

**Reader's change:** She has a distinct represented concern, community and theological response. Her enslaved status in the frame differentiates her social position; it does not establish ownership by Perpetua or provide a complete personal history. **Interaction:** none. A substantial passage commands the screen, under **The narrator describes Felicity**. A source note handles the legal statement without claiming a general Roman law finding.

**Open question:** How should a reader meet a hope that may unsettle their own expectation of what this woman should want? **Transition:** the narrator leads both women toward the arena. The narrative does not ask the reader to choose whose maternal response is better.

## 8. She gives her a hand

**Primary:** §§18–21, particularly §20. Wallis VI.1–4.

> she approached and gave her her hand, and lifted her up. And both of them stood together

**Situation and tension:** After the women are thrown down, the narrator represents Perpetua helping Felicity stand. The text later describes their deaths. State that plainly in concise curatorial copy, with the complete ending independently accessible and a clear notice that it describes violence. Do not use the offered hand to imply escape or a happy ending.

**Reader's change:** These are two people in a narrated act together, rather than two examples of a source limitation. The narrator's language of victory and the physical violence remain different aspects of the same ending. **Interaction:** no reenactment or forced reveal. **Read the narrator's complete ending (§§18–21)** is optional; no cliffhanger conceals the outcome. **Voice boundary:** all ending language belongs to the narrator, including represented final speech and interpretation of agency. Perpetua is not reporting her own death.

**Open question:** What does the work's ending ask a reader to remember about these women? The page can end after that question and the source link. A creator note awaits question 5; it is not filled by Codex. Existing Carthage, Pliny and Scripture exits can remain available after the reading without turning the close into a collection of destinations.

## Pacing, motifs and ethical limits

The scene/sequel movement is practical change in §3 → interpreted expectation in §4 → family appeal and judgment in §§5–6 → request/refusal and relief in §6 → prayer and family return in §§7–9 → a change of speaker → Felicity's represented hope → the narrator's ending. The treatment restores that movement rather than imposing eight equal panels. The source sequence is not evidence that its composition was contemporaneous with the events.

Care and nourishment connect the nursing infant, received food and inaccessible water. Hands appear in the father's appeal and the narrator's final gesture. These are proposed close-reading motifs, not proof of authorial intention or historical causation. Avoid drawing a literal milk-to-water animation or implying that one child replaces another.

Let the writing set the length of each movement. No mandatory diagram, selector, disclosure and Continue arrangement. Deliberate silence means no score, no invented prison ambience and no automatic advancement; it does not mean blank screens the reader must wait through. An open-ended question must arise from a passage, not appear as a compulsory solemn caption on every screen.

The reviewed production architecture can later carry this reading while preserving old fragment aliases, explicit speaker labels, full-reading/no-JS access, keyboard focus and source gates. That integration is **not implemented or authorized as a production replacement by this research**. The only current proof uses native HTML disclosure outside the application. All historical/theological wording remains a draft for qualified human review; technical success cannot establish narrative success.
