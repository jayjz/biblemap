## Recovery pass: the hearing, vision, Felicity and final frame

The November 4 recovery builds on the staged six-movement implementation. The stronger source dossier at `docs/editorial/02-primary-source-dossier.md` supersedes the older narrative audit's statement that Muehlberger was unread: it records complete article access and page-specific findings. The creator profile also preserves actual submissions, beyond the single excerpt noticed in the interrupted audit. No creator submissions are imitated or newly published here; all added summaries and interpretive notes remain editorial drafts.

The hearing now pairs the father's appeal with his physical gestures and gives the daughter’s reply comparable scale to the other appeals. Her narrated grief at his beating follows the refusal. Tertullian's *Apology* 29–32 adds a specific theological/political distinction: he describes prayer for emperors while rejecting sacrifice. This is his polemical Christian defense, not independent evidence of this hearing. S. Thelwall, *ANF* III (1885), New Advent revision: https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0301.htm. Questions about obligations to parent, child and conscience arise from these conflicting claims; no philosopher is added to dictate a verdict.

The vision replaces the branch-only drawing with an original AI-generated interpretive image and three selectable exact Wallis excerpts: expected beasts, the changed body, and waking interpretation. All three remain in the static/no-JS reading. The quoted phrase “became a man” and the trainer's later “Daughter” remain uncollapsed. This is a reading encounter, not a combat simulation. Passion §4 (ANF I.3) supplies an earlier shift from expecting escape to expecting martyrdom; §10 (III.2) supplies the vision and its waking interpretation. New notes do not claim a universal gender reading or supernatural verification.

Felicity's main text restores the narrator's account of her fear of being left behind and her companions' grief, childbirth, the attendant's challenge and another woman's care for the daughter (§15 / ANF V.2). The different social descriptions in §2 / ANF I.1 are accessible; no ownership relationship between Perpetua and Felicity is invented. The Ravenna figure is no longer displayed: the Visual Museum calls it Felicitas of Rome (https://www.visualmuseum.gallery/gallery/six-women-martyrs-in-ravenna/). Its acquired file and provenance are preserved as an unused alternative. It is replaced by Kollwitz's *The Mothers*, 1922, a modern artistic parallel from the Library of Congress (LCCN 2009630850; cph.3a42742). Exact Commons file record documents the LOC statement and public-domain basis. It does not depict these prisoners. Worcester's reproduction could not be acquired, and the Met's current facsimile record restricts its image, so neither reproduction is used.

The final narrator's account of one woman raising the other (§20 / ANF VI.3) restores a concrete human action between the writing's end and the deaths. Sources, authorship disagreement, navigation and outward routes precede the last question. This route omits the shared footer. Muehlberger, “Perpetual Adjustment,” *JECS* 30.3 (2022), 313–342, especially 315–318 and 333–342, is placed beside Shewring's *Passion* (1931), introduction xviii–xx. The former argues for literary composition; the latter argues for distinct authentic accounts from style and rhythm. This is an unresolved disagreement, not an expert vote or a consensus assertion. Sources and access limits remain in the existing dossier.

Each movement has a different compositional role, and the final narrator's act of one woman lifting the other precedes the question about whose voice survives. Theology is encountered in the source's own language and the sacrifice/prayer distinction; philosophical questions arise from obligations and testimony rather than prestigious names. The largest remaining visual weakness is the hearing's dependence on translated typography, particularly its long phone reading.
