# Jonah: the lives beyond the protagonist

Curatorial and artistic proposal, 30 October 2026. Research and design judgment by Codex, not the creator’s personal testimony or human editorial approval. Starting revision: `2de3b7d`; main: `60ecbda`. Work stays in `feat/jonah-human-experience`.

## The encounter

Jonah is a short book with an unusually large human horizon. A command names a city. A departure puts strangers aboard the same ship. A prayer speaks from confinement. The command returns. People change their actions; the prophet objects. Finally, concern extends to people and animals, and the book stops before Jonah answers. These are observations of the narrative, not a diagnosis of Jonah or a claim that every reader must draw the same lesson.

The artistic proposition is **a change in the scale of attention**. At first, a person and a destination; then a crew; then an entire city; finally the fragile shade over one head beside the lives of many. Preserve the irritation, comedy, danger, bodily discomfort, and theological difficulty together. Do not make the fish the book’s destination, turn the sailors into scenery, or make Nineveh stand for an undifferentiated evil population.

Let the visitor enter before asking them to interpret. In the review entrance, the book’s name, four chapters, translation, and Begin reading are sufficient. The existing personal invitations are withheld in this opt-in study because they frame the departure as avoidance before Jonah 4:2. Existing author slots and approved words remain in the source. Human recognition can begin with a ship’s fare, other people trying to survive, a home changing hands, or shade being lost. We cannot claim that it actually does without observing visitors.

## The whole thirteen-scene rhythm

This is the direction for the complete book, not a claim that thirteen scenes were redesigned.

| Existing scene | Curatorial rhythm / medium | Discovery and boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1:1–2 Calling | A brief opening, the complete command, a destination that will return at the end. Preserve Raphael as optional Renaissance reception. | Nineveh is named before Jonah’s motive. No psychological subtitle is necessary in the review entrance. |
| 1:3 Flight | Geography and the material act of buying passage. Widen toward the sea; keep Joppa locatable in the existing atlas. | Tarshish stays unplotted and disputed. Roberts’s 1839 Jaffa is a later landscape, not an ancient port reconstruction. |
| 1:4–6 Wind | Change the pace through the contrast between the crew’s actions and Jonah’s sleep. No simulated storm required. | Backhuysen can be encountered as a 1701 sea image, not Jonah’s vessel. Silence allows the captain’s words to interrupt. |
| 1:7–10 Lots | Reduce the visual field; the sailors’ questions and Jonah’s identification carry the encounter. | No fabricated interview, face, dice animation, or reconstructed lot ritual. |
| 1:11–16 Sea | Give the attempt to row ashore time before the casting overboard. Sadeler’s complete print is a later interpretation with narrative compression. | The crew’s reluctance belongs to the text. Do not skip it for spectacle. |
| 1:17 Depths | The sudden change of scale is enough. Retain exact WEB numbering. | Hebrew 2:1 corresponds to WEB 1:17. A marine illustration cannot verify species or survival. |
| 2:1–9 Prayer | A longer, narrower reading measure: the sustained voice after many short exchanges. | Optional Christian sign-of-Jonah reading distinguishes Matthew from Luke; no automatic harmonization or resurrection proof. |
| 2:10 Shore | One abrupt bodily sentence, followed by space. | Gassel’s Renaissance release appears only after the reader reaches release. No invented landing place. |
| 3:1–4 Return | Recall the first command, now read again with changed action. The existing atlas provides orientation. | Geography is shareable; the book does not name an Assyrian king or date the visit. |
| 3:5–10 City | Expand from individual to population, then pause at 3:10. **Implemented study.** A legal record supplies particular human names without assigning them to Jonah’s audience. | A second route into the material: recorded property relationships rather than a panoramic city nobody can verify. |
| 4:1–4 Response | Preserve the chapter cut and let Jonah give his own reason. **Implemented study.** | Aquinas remains a medieval Catholic thematic connection, not commentary on Jonah or a universal answer. |
| 4:5–10 Shade | The early Christian marble is a still moment set against the changing text. The raised arm and leaves invite looking. | Compare Islamic manuscript foliage only in a clearly attributed reception encounter. Do not settle the Hebrew plant’s species through art. |
| 4:11 Question | Return to the city’s name with the full final verse. Leave substantial space and an ordinary way out. | Rashi and Radak differ over the animals. Do not append a moral verdict, quiz, affirmation, or synthetic answer. #32 remains a separate integration option. |

## Readings without flattening traditions

The research record distinguishes the Hebrew book, rabbinic argument, medieval Jewish commentary, Christian Gospel reception and typology, medieval Catholic theological reasoning, and Qur’anic accounts. These are not steps in a single evolving interpretation. The Qur’an is scripture in its own tradition, not a gloss on a Christian Bible. Matthew’s comparison with three days and Luke’s emphasis on Jonah as a sign must be read in their own passages. Tacitus and Josephus are later non-biblical historical writings with limited evidentiary reach; they do not independently attest Jonah’s sign saying or a resurrection. Augustine’s Christian typology can also carry anti-Jewish contrasts: do not quietly present that framework as neutral history.

The dossier includes nonconfessional literary observations, not an invented secular consensus. Wider Syriac, Eastern Christian, modern Jewish, Muslim tafsir, feminist, ecological and postcolonial specialist reception remains a research opportunity, not a completed survey.

## Evaluation independent of the maker

Recruit six first-time readers, including people unfamiliar with Jonah and people with different or no religious commitments. A facilitator who did not design this version should run counterbalanced sessions with the ordinary reader and this study, using the same device and passages. Do not explain the creative thesis beforehand.

Ask participants to begin Jonah, read through to 4:4, inspect something that interests them, and return to Scripture. Observe navigation hesitations, missed verses, exits, use of sources, and reading interruptions. Afterwards ask: “What happened?”, “What was the Nineveh record?”, “Were those people in Jonah’s story?”, “Which parts were ancient evidence, later interpretation, or design?”, and “What would you want to explore next?” Ask what was unclear and what felt imposed. Record indifference and inaccurate recollection as well as interest; do not infer feelings from time on page. Separately, have an Assyriologist check the transaction/date/transliteration, a Hebrew reader check the linguistic notes, and Jewish, Christian and Muslim readers review descriptions of their particular sources. A human screen-reader session and real-device test are still needed.

No participants or independent experts are available in this session. No reactions, comprehension improvement, spiritual benefit or emotional success are claimed. Browser tests establish narrower technical observations only.
