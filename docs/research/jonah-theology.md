# Jonah — theology and interpretation archive

Retrieval date: **2026-10-10**. Research baseline: `feat/jonah-reader` at `bd0d948ecee642f6adfc89a9c380b95290ee1e3b`.

Scripture is the primary text. Everything else here is reception: ancient Christian commentary, medieval Jewish commentary, one scholastic catena, and a few modern names that were checked and mostly **not** quoted. Short exact quotations are collected in [jonah-quotations.md](jonah-quotations.md). This file does not write narration, reflection prompts, or an author's reading.

Labels: **Verified** (the page was opened and supports the claim), **Disputed** (sources that were opened do not agree), **Interpretive** (a named reader's lens, not a fact about the narrative), **Unverified** (not publishable as a quotation or as a consensus).

## Purpose and scope

The questions a later author might actually have to face:

- Why does Jonah run?
- What is being opposed when he is angry: justice, or mercy?
- What did Nineveh's repentance consist of, and was the warning "forty days" or "three"?
- Why does the book end on a question (Jonah 4:11)?
- In what sense, if any, is Jonah a sign of Christ — and who refuses to let that reading swallow the story?

No single commentator below is "the" interpretation. Agreement between Jerome and Rashi on one motive is interesting. It is not a consensus of Judaism and Christianity, and it is not what the narrative itself states.

## The disagreements worth keeping

### 1. Why he flees

The narrator of chapter 1 says he fled "from the presence of the LORD" and does not say why. Chapter 4 puts a reason in Jonah's mouth: he knew God was gracious and would relent.

Opened readers do not say the same thing:

- **Josephus:** fear. He also changes the sermon into a political threat and stops before repentance.
- **Rashi and Radak:** he hoped prophecy would not follow him outside the land, and he did not want a gentile repentance to indict Israel. They are explaining a traditional question. Rashi cites the *Mekhilta*. They are not claiming the narrator stated this in 1:3.
- **Jerome:** on 1:3, the Spirit shows the prophet that the nations' repentance is Israel's ruin; Jonah is *amator patriae suae*. On 4:1 he rejects another view: "Non igitur contristatur, ut quidam putant, quod gentium multitudo salvetur; sed quod pereat Israel." Rendering: he is not, as some think, grieved that a multitude of the nations is saved, but that Israel perishes. **Augustine, Letter 102.35**, says Jonah "was grieved at the salvation of the Ninevites, that is, at the redemption and deliverance of the Gentiles." Read flatly, those sentences conflict. A harmonizing reading (grief at Gentile salvation *because* it dooms Israel) is possible and is not what either sentence says. This pass does not claim Jerome was answering Letter 102. New Advent dates that letter 409; Jerome's year was not fixed from a chronology page opened here. Jerome's prologue also calls Jonah a type of the Savior. That claim and the "Israel perishes" claim sit in one book. They are not the same claim.
- **Chrysostom:** flight is futile and spreads harm to the sailors. He is preaching, not reconstructing a motive the text withheld.

A psychological story about avoidance or vocation is a modern analogy. It is not established by any source opened here.

### 2. Mercy, justice, and whether the oracle failed

Jonah 4, in the WEB, has God ask, "Is it right for you to be angry?" Jerome says the Hebrew of that question can be read as anger or as grief.

On the threat itself, three opened Christian texts do not agree:

- **Jerome** on 3:4 prefers the Hebrew forty days and says the Septuagint's three "does not suit repentance."
- **Augustine, *City of God* 18.44**, prefers the Hebrew too — "I rather think what is read in the Hebrew, Yet forty days" — but he defends the Greek three as different and still pertinent, and tells the reader not to despise either.
- **Chrysostom** preaches "Yet three days" as the sentence Nineveh heard. He says God preferred that his own prediction fall rather than the city, and then immediately qualifies: "Or rather, the prophecy did not even so fall to the ground."

**Augustine, *City of God* 21.24**, goes further than the number: the prediction was fulfilled, because wicked Nineveh was overthrown in its manners while the walls stood. That disagrees with Chrysostom's "let the prediction fall." It also disagrees with a flat reading of Jonah 3 in the WEB, where God sees their work and relents of the disaster. Augustine is redefining what "overthrown" meant. Label it his reading.

### 3. Nineveh's repentance

WEB Jonah 3, opened on eBible: the people believe God, fast, and put on sackcloth; the king rises, puts on sackcloth, and sits in ashes. The warning on that page is "In forty days, Nineveh will be overthrown!" The text does not say the repentance lasted, or that an Assyrian inscription records it. No such inscription was opened.

Chrysostom praises repentance "on uncertain grounds": no "if you repent" clause was attached. He is using the Greek number, so the urgency he praises is the Greek book's urgency.

Megillah 31a is why Jonah is the Yom Kippur afternoon haftarah in the Babylonian rite that page records: because of Nineveh's repentance. That is liturgy, not a historical finding about Assyria.

### 4. The last question

WEB Jonah 4:11 is a question: whether God should not be concerned for Nineveh, "more than one hundred twenty thousand persons who can't discern between their right hand and their left hand, and also many animals." The verse does not define the 120,000.

Jerome, reaching this verse, says the tropological reading is extremely difficult, and that one attempt to solve it by addressing the words to the Son falls into blasphemy. That is an ancient commentator marking a limit. Rashi's English on Sefaria, which was seen and not copied, glosses the phrase as children and takes "beasts" in a metaphorical sense. That English is copyrighted. The disagreement (animals in the WEB and in Jerome's Latin lemma, versus a metaphorical reading) is real enough to flag, and the copyrighted sentences are not reproduced.

Augustine allegorized the gourd and the worm in Letter 102. He did not, in the sections opened, expound the 120,000.

### 5. Christological reading, and its limits

- Matthew 12:39–41 makes the three days a sign of the Son of Man. That is canonical for Christians. It is still a reading of Jonah, not a line in Jonah.
- Augustine, *City of God* 18.30, says the fish prophesied the death and resurrection more clearly than speech would have. Letter 102.34 adds ship/cross and storm/world. Letter 102.37, on the same page, says it is not lawful to interpret the three days otherwise than Christ did. He is strict about that one point and freer about the gourd.
- Jerome's prologue states the type in one sentence. Before the storm he limits it: "Jonas propheta non absque periculo interpretantis, totus referri ad Dominum poterit." The Gospel citation does not make every scene a Christ-scene. His doubt about the LXX's three days at 3:4, and his trouble at 4:11, belong with that limit.
- Aquinas's catena shows fathers arguing whether the three days were full days, and quotes Chrysostom insisting the fish happened "in deed," against a death that was only an appearance. Those arguments are not the same as Jerome's ban on a total allegory.
- Jewish readers opened here (Rashi, Radak, Ibn Ezra, Megillah 31a) do not make Jonah a type of Christ.
- Josephus keeps the fish, drops the repentance, and never mentions a sign of a future resurrection.

"Ancient readers understood Jonah as a sign of the resurrection" is true of Matthew, Augustine, and Jerome's prologue. It is false as a description of Josephus, Rashi, or of Jerome's own warning against referring the whole book to the Lord.
