Paul's letters are early Christian first-person communications, transmitted through later manuscripts, not surviving autographs. Mark and Acts are literary biblical narratives; authorship, dating and the relationship between Acts and Pauline letters are debated. This prototype does not count them as independent confirmations of each detail or fix their composition to exact years. Their canonical status and their historical genre are different questions.

Josephus wrote *Antiquities* around 93/94 CE, after the events. His surviving account is not a deposition from the trial. Greek manuscripts, ancient quotations and Latin transmission mediate our access. The received James notice is often accepted as authentic; interpolation arguments exist. [T. C. Schmidt, *Josephus and Jesus* (2025), Appendix 2, pp. 231–248](https://academic.oup.com/book/60034/chapter/513641505) was inspected for its discussion, including Origen's mismatched references and the transmission arguments. It advocates authenticity; that position is not neutral consensus by decree. [Nicholas P. L. Allen (2017), publisher abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/2222582X.2017.1317008) argues for interpolation; only the abstract was consulted. The experience attributes its death account to the received text and exposes this dispute. Approximately 62 CE is a chronological inference from the governor succession, not a date written in 20.200. Whiston's bracketed 61 is not silently treated as Josephus's date.

Eusebius 2.23.19–20 also attributes a connection between James's death and Jerusalem's catastrophe to Josephus. That claim is absent from the extant *Antiquities* passage. It is not imported into the Josephus layer. Hegesippus's closing compression toward Vespasian's siege and the expanded death scene are not combined with Josephus into a precise itinerary. Independent authorship, independent information, and multiple surviving copies must be distinguished.

## Narrative spine, approved for neither publication nor theology

James enters through a relationship: a brother named in a Gospel, a person Paul says he met. There is no recoverable childhood scene. Paul's resurrection testimony then names an appearance to James in a single clause, without furnishing an encounter we can stage. The record widens into relationships: James, Peter/Cephas, John, Paul, Gentile believers and a Jerusalem community negotiating fellowship and observance. Josephus shifts attention to a death and the contested exercise of authority. Finally, later Christian remembrance expands the spare record into a just man, a praying ascetic and a martyr. The ending returns to the difference between a remembered person and the many voices through which we meet him. The title is an artistic framing of his continuing Jerusalem presence in the sources, not a claim that he never traveled, alone remained loyal, or outlived every other disciple.
