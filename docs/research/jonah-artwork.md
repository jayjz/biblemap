# Jonah artwork — source archive

Retrieval date: **2026-10-10**. Research baseline: `feat/jonah-reader` at `bd0d948ecee642f6adfc89a9c380b95290ee1e3b`.

This file records candidate images for a human editor. It does not approve any asset, attach anything to `src/content/jonah.ts`, or download files. An earlier short list lives in [docs/jonah-art-research.md](../jonah-art-research.md). Items from that list that were not re-opened in this pass are marked as such.

## Purpose and scope

Find depictions of Jonah, and a few contextual images of the sea or of Assyria, whose holding institution, title, and rights can be checked. Ancient subject matter is not a license. The copyright in a painting and the rights in a particular photograph or scan are different things.

Labels used below:

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Verified | Catalog facts were read from the institution's page, or from that page's indexed text when a direct fetch was blocked and the limitation is stated. |
| Rights-open | The object page, not merely a sitewide footer, says the image may be copied without permission. |
| Rights-unconfirmed | The object is identified. Do not download until a human reads the badge on that page. |
| Contextual | Not a depiction of Jonah. |
| Incomplete this pass | Recorded earlier in the repo, or only partly re-checked. |

## Five strongest candidates

Nonbinding. These are the clearest fits for a reader who must not pretend a later picture is an eyewitness view. None is approved for production.

1. **[Jonah Swallowed](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1965.237)** (Cleveland Museum of Art, 1965.237). Late Roman marble, Anatolia, 280–90 CE. The museum's own sentence: "Here, the beast swallows Jonah headfirst." It says "sea monster," not a whale species. Possible placement: the end of Jonah 1, beside the text's "huge fish," with the caption stating that this is early Christian reception, not a reconstruction of an ancient ship. **Rights-open.**
2. **[Jonah Cast Up](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1965.238)** (1965.238). Same group and date. The beast "spits Jonah out." Possible placement: after the prayer in the fish, not as an illustration of 1:17, which ends with Jonah still inside. The existing art note already warns against that substitution. **Rights-open.**
3. **[Jonah Under the Gourd Vine](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1965.239)** (1965.239). Jonah at rest after Nineveh is spared, then the vine withers. Possible placement: Jonah 4 only. The plant's species is a separate textual problem (see the theology file); this sculpture chooses a vine. **Rights-open.**
4. **[Jonah Praying](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1965.240)** (1965.240). The museum says the moment is unclear: prayer inside the beast, a plea for Nineveh's destruction, or grief over the gourd. Possible placement: only with that uncertainty left visible. Do not assign it to one scene in a caption that the object page refuses to make. **Rights-open.**
5. **[Table Base with Jonah Swallowed and Cast Up by the Big Fish](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/468759)** (The Met, 77.7). Roman marble, early 300s, made in Asia Minor. The museum describes a *ketos* (a classical sea monster), cites Matthew 12:40, and calls the ship "a rare detailed depiction of a Roman merchant ship." It also says the base was found at Tarsos. That findspot is the object's archaeology. It is not evidence that biblical Tarshish was Tarsus. Possible placement: storm and fish as one early Christian object, with the ship labeled Roman, not Phoenician. **Rights-unconfirmed** (see Met note below).

First alternate, if the editor wants a picture that is not Christian typology: the Met's ca. 1400 Iranian folio ["Jonah and the Whale"](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/453683), which the museum ties to the Qur'an (37:139) and shows Jonah after the fish, under a gourd, with an angel. Same rights caution as other Met pages.

## Rights, in one place

**Cleveland.** Each of the four Jonah marbles' pages says: "You can copy, modify, and distribute this work, all without asking permission." The museum's [open-access statement](https://www.clevelandart.org/open-access) publishes images of public-domain works and collection metadata under [CC0](https://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/zero/1.0/). Attribution is not legally required under CC0; naming the museum, title, and accession is still the honest editorial practice. No image URL was copied into this archive. Download from the object page when a human approves the asset. The CMA dataset on GitHub is metadata; it is not a substitute for looking at the sculpture.

The four marbles are part of a larger group. A CMA page index for *Jonah Praying* reports William Wixom's statement that an **unconfirmed** report placed 1965.237–247 together in one pithos. The findspot is not secure. A Good Shepherd in the same acquisition is not a Jonah image and is not listed above.

**The Met.** Object records were read from collection pages. Those pages also print a sitewide footer: open-access data and public-domain images may be used without permission or fee, while images under copyright go through an image-request form. That footer appears on more than one kind of record. It is **not** treated here as an object-level "Public Domain" badge. Before any Met file is saved into `public/media`, someone must read the badge on that object and record the download URL, date, and checksum, as [docs/jonah-art-research.md](../jonah-art-research.md) already requires.

**Rijksmuseum.** [Information and Data Policy](https://data.rijksmuseum.nl/policy/): many objects are marked Public Domain or CC0 on the collection page; some are CC BY 4.0; some are not free. [Image requests](https://www.rijksmuseum.nl/en/research/image-requests) asks for the credit "Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam." This pass did not capture the notice on the three Jonah prints. Do not assume CC0.

**National Gallery of Art.** Open data for facts is CC0 and **does not include image files**. The Raphael sheet's "public domain" line comes from the search index of the artwork page, after a 403 on a direct fetch. Re-open [the object](https://www.nga.gov/artworks/74167-prophets-hosea-and-jonah).

**British Museum.** The Nineveh relief is contextual only. Nothing in this pass authorizes copying BM photography.

**Not recommended.** [commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dore_jonah_whale.jpg](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dore_jonah_whale.jpg) is a 412-pixel scan whose license template still asks for a United States public-domain tag. Gustave Doré died in 1883, so the *plate* is old, but this file is not a cleared reproduction. A museum or a properly tagged Commons file would be a different decision.

## What these images are not

- They are not evidence of what a ship at Joppa looked like, or of a biological whale. The World English Bible, pinned for Jonah 1 in this repo, says "huge fish." Cleveland says "sea monster." The Met table base says *ketos*. Later European prints say "whale." Those are reception choices.
- A lamassu from Nimrud is not a picture of Nineveh, and a Nineveh palace relief is not a picture of the book of Jonah.
- Barry's print is Michelangelo's prophet as reproduced in 1801, not the Sistine ceiling and not a photograph of it. Vatican photography of the ceiling was not cleared and is not listed.

## Editorial opportunities (nonbinding)

- The four Cleveland marbles can carry a whole chapter arc — swallowed, praying (if the uncertainty is kept), cast up, vine — from one late-third-century group, with one rights regime.
- The Met table base is the strongest single object for "ship plus creature" if the Met badge is in fact public domain.
- The ca. 1400 Iranian folio is the cleanest way to show that Jonah/Yunus is not only a Latin Christian subject.
- Rijksmuseum's Van de Velde series, if the other three states can be identified and the copyright lines are actually open, would be a narrative cycle in one workshop. Only the "thrown into the sea" plate was opened.
- Do not use the gourd sculpture, the Vernet "leaving the whale," or the de Vos "cast on shore" print inside the Jonah 1 reader. Jonah 1 ends in the fish.
