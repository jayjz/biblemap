# Thomas: source dossier and uncertainty ledger

Checked 5 November 2026. AI-assisted research and editorial draft; human historical, theological, translation and literary review pending. This dossier authorizes no production release. Scope: John's Thomas narrative and a separate manuscript/sayings doorway. Evidence states describe bounded claims, never the truth of resurrection or a reader's belief.

## Research method and retained evidence

Read the sources below through Firecrawl and inspect the publisher HTML retained under `src/content/scripture/thomas-web-*.html`. Eight WEB chapters, retrieved 2026-11-05, are immutable build inputs. `scripts/pin-thomas.mjs --check` extracts 19 selected verses, removes footnote markup, normalizes whitespace (including line-end spaces in the retained HTML) and checks SHA-256 against each complete retained chapter. No network runs in builds. `scripts/acquire-thomas.mjs` is an explicit research ingestion command, not a build command. The retained Mattison record contains one saying and the translator's rights dedication. Website design and modern scholarly annotations are not assumed to share ancient-text rights.

The machine-readable registry is `src/content/thomas.ts`. Stable source IDs below match its records. Ledger states: SUPPORTED = attested in the specified text/catalogue; INFERRED = a reasoned reading beyond its wording; CONFLICTED = competing credible readings; UNRESOLVED = missing evidence. No numerical probability is assigned. Modern translations are access witnesses, not ancient physical manuscripts. A manuscript copy date supplies a boundary for a text already circulating, not its author's identity or original composition date.

## A. Canonical Thomas

John is the narrative source: a composed Gospel transmitted through manuscripts, not a modern interview transcript or Thomas's memoir. The prototype does not settle authorship or impose an exact composition year. Thomas is one of Jesus's disciples, named among the Twelve. John's scene follows Jesus's death, Mary's report and an appearance to gathered disciples (20:1–23). The immediate setting is disciples gathered indoors; 20:24–29 specifies neither a recoverable street address nor a room plan. Do not silently identify a tourist site or stage an archaeologically authenticated upper room.

## C. Gospel of Thomas: physical witnesses, composition, transmission

The noncanonical collection is conventionally divided into 114 sayings, rather than a continuous passion/resurrection narrative. Numbering is an editorial reference system. Its prologue is an attribution, not evidence that Thomas wrote it. The separate doorway must never function as a sixth scene of John.

T17 SUPPORTED: these physical records attest circulation of related Greek sayings before the surviving Coptic copy. T18 SUPPORTED: differences in order/content mean one should not mechanically reconstruct missing Greek from Coptic. See [L. W. Hurtado, “The Greek Fragments … as Artefacts”](https://larryhurtado.files.wordpress.com/2010/07/gthomas-greek-fragments.pdf), published 2008, pp.19–32, sections on the three papyri and concluding observations (`thomas-hurtado`), author-hosted preprint inspected. His copy-use inferences are hypotheses, not direct observations of ancient reading behavior. No full article or facsimiles redistributed.

T19 CONFLICTED: composition, growth and dependence remain disputed. [April D. DeConick, “The Gospel of Thomas,” §§2–3](https://repository.rice.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/e001a3e4-3823-4050-8030-d7c8befc2f89/content), Rice-hosted author essay (`thomas-deconick`), argues a developing collection with early traditions. [Simeon Burke, “When was the Gospel of Thomas written?”](https://www.bibleodyssey.org/articles/when-was-the-gospel-of-thomas-written/) (2022; `thomas-dating`) presents a later dating and reports Goodacre/Gathercole's case for knowledge of the Synoptics. Their books were not read in full; do not imply otherwise. Earlier layers, final collection and surviving copies ask different dating questions. Similarity alone establishes neither direction of dependence nor historical authenticity. Neither scholarly approach authenticates each saying as Jesus's words. Modern scholarship is copyrighted, linked and briefly paraphrased.

T20 CONFLICTED: “Gnostic” is not a sufficient description of the entire collection. DeConick challenges blanket classification. This prototype uses “noncanonical sayings collection,” leaving theological taxonomy open. It does not characterize all sayings as secret historical facts suppressed by a monolithic Church.

Source caution: Burke's discussion ties an argument about saying 71 to a temple-destruction chronology with problematic 132–136 CE wording. The Second Temple was destroyed in 70 CE; the prototype does not repeat that sentence or use it to date the collection. His article is evidence of one scholarly position, not an uncontested timeline. Search excerpts and summaries are not independent corroborating witnesses.

## Translation and one bounded comparison

T21 SUPPORTED: [Mark M. Mattison's translation](https://www.gospels.net/thomas) expressly dedicates its text to the public domain and identifies NHC II,2 as its basis. A retained JSON excerpt records saying 26 and the dedication; the website's photographs/design are not thereby licensed. Only this saying is ingested for display. It is **a translation of the Coptic witness**, not a translation of the fragmentary P.Oxy. 1. Translation choices and correspondence require human philological review; no Coptic/Greek collation is claimed.

T22 SUPPORTED as resemblance: saying 26 and [Luke 6:41–42](https://ebible.org/engwebp/LUK06.htm) share the speck/beam image and the movement toward clear sight. Compare also Matthew 7:3–5 (linked for future study, not newly quoted). T23 UNRESOLVED: which literary/oral path produced that relationship. The comparison permits close reading without a “same source” badge, authenticity score or reconstructed priority. It belongs on `/human-story/thomas/sayings/`, reached after an explicit change of source. It is not John's answer to doubt and is not attributed to the historical apostle as author.

## Narrative spine and review boundary

An unfamiliar visitor meets a disciple in John's account, after Jesus's death. Others report an appearance Thomas missed. He sets a condition. The Gospel leaves an interval, then gathers him with the others and brings Jesus into the narrative. An invitation is followed by Thomas's confession, with no touch described. Jesus's blessing turns beyond the witnessed encounter toward those who have not seen. The prototype ends there; sources and the unrelated sayings doorway follow as optional exploration. None of the connective prose is approved creator-authored theology.
