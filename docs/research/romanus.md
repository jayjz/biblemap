## Narrative foundation (written before staging)

Romanus belonged to the Christian church around Caesarea, on the eastern Mediterranean coast. Eusebius calls him a deacon and exorcist. His account places Romanus in Antioch during the persecution associated with Diocletian. Churches were being demolished; Romanus saw men, women and children sacrificing to the gods and publicly rebuked them. The account gives us that action, not his childhood, private thoughts or the crowd's individual reasons.

Sacrifice could join religious practice to public obedience. Eusebius's opening distinguishes successive measures against churches, clergy and eventually the wider population. The short Romanus chapter does not establish that every person in that particular crowd was under an immediate legal order. The longer Syriac text explicitly describes compulsion. The difference matters.

In the shorter Greek account, arrest leads to a sentence of burning. At the stake, before the fire is lit, Romanus asks where the fire is. The sentence changes: his tongue is removed. He is imprisoned and eventually strangled as an imperial anniversary and release of prisoners approach. Eusebius interprets his endurance as divine strength. His text supplies no speech after the mutilation.

The longer text preserved in Syriac does report renewed speech as God's gift. That is a difference within the transmission of Eusebius, not merely a century-later author adding a miracle to an unchanged original. Later preaching and poetry make the speaking martyr central. Chrysostom's accepted homily interprets the voice as spiritual victory and begins with love; Prudentius turns Romanus into an extended Latin speaker against traditional cults, asking him for poetic eloquence. Their reported speeches cannot be treated as a recovered transcript. Romanus's life ends, but writers continue to give him words. The closing question concerns what preservation and retelling do to the person remembered.

## Decisions that affect the encounter

- Compare sources rather than count them as independent witnesses. Dependencies among homilies, passions and poem are partly inferred and contested.
- Use c. 303 for the death, with precise-day uncertainty in the source disclosure. The short text connects it with Alphaeus and Zacchaeus (17 November); the Syriac OCR reads seventh Teshri. Later commemoration on 18 November is a separate reception issue. Do not build an exact-day timeline.
- The short account leaves the emperor unnamed; Syriac names Diocletian; Prudentius names Galerius in his opening setting. McGiffert argues for Galerius. Tsartsidis offers a different reconciliation of trial/death dates. Do not place a named emperor physically at Antioch in a supposedly settled chronology.
- Original rhetorical drawing, map and typography are modern AI-assisted compositions; no likeness, ancient manuscript or verified reconstruction. No simulated miracle, graphic injury or synthesized speech.
- Alternatives considered: reconstructed street drama would manufacture appearances; an archival gallery would interrupt causality. Chosen direction is a continuous illustrated reading with a persistent shorter text and expandable textual leaves. Violet/ochre tones distinguish the written remembrance from the stone of Daphne. Georgia carries narrative and display; Arial carries controls. The central gesture is the additional writing, not movement.
- Final proposed question: **When we keep someone's voice alive, how much of what survives is theirs—and how much is ours?** This is modern editorial reflection, not an ancient quotation.
