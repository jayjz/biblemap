# Three encounters, three forms

Narrative treatments for PR #36, November 1, 2026. These supersede the collection-first emphasis of the earlier portal study. The five worlds remain the surrounding navigation. This document designs three encounters; the first implementation is **Perpetua — The Prison**. It changes the existing Perpetua route, rather than adding a destination count.

See the [visual storyboard sheets](storyboards/human-story-narratives.html) and [claim and lineage audit](research/perpetua-narrative-audit.md). All new editorial writing is a draft for human review. The treatments describe intended effects, not observed visitor reactions.

## Perpetua — The Prison

### Dramatic center

We encounter Perpetua through a text that attributes an account to her, then carries on after she can no longer write. The opening situation is particular: a nursing mother in custody, trying to care for her infant. Her family, Christian identity and the power to condemn her enter the same account. The visitor never plays her, supplies her thoughts or decides her fate.

The surviving Passion is a Christian martyr narrative, not a neutral court transcript. Its editorial frame introduces a group, attributes §§3–10 to Perpetua and §§11–13 to Saturus, and resumes narration in §14. Its authorial claims are themselves part of the evidence. Felicity is described by a narrator; she has no corresponding first-person account here. Visions belong to the source's religious testimony, not independently established views of another world.

### Six questions

| Question | Treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Whose story? | Perpetua, with her infant, father and companions; Felicity appears as another person whose access to us is differently mediated. |
| What survives? | A transmitted, edited martyr narrative with attributed first-person passages, reported speech, visions and an ending supplied by another voice. |
| Stakes inside the narrative? | Care for a child, pressure from a father, refusal to sacrifice, condemnation and separation. The Christian narrator understands martyrdom as victory; the visitor can inspect that interpretation. |
| What changes? | The child first stays with her, then is withheld; a family appeal becomes a tribunal encounter; attributed writing gives way to someone else's account. |
| What can interaction reveal? | Selecting a relationship exposes where that person appears and where their own words are absent. A source register locates every scene in the composite work; changing scenes makes the handover of voice visible. Report and interpretation can be examined separately without replacing either. |
| What remains? | How closely can we encounter a person when her voice survives through a text that also interprets her death? What cannot we recover about her child and Felicity? |

### Scene score and source boundaries

1. **The child in the prison.** Begin within §3, with care rather than spectacle. Large off-center title; two linked, abstract forms in a wide field. Brief original paraphrase says she reports nursing the hungry infant and obtaining permission for him to stay. Relationship selectors: child, family, deacons. Each opens a specific source observation. The forms are a modern diagram of narrated access, not human likenesses or a prison plan.
2. **A father at the tribunal.** §§5–6. The father and the official both appeal to family; she continues to identify as Christian and is condemned. The visitor switches between **Perpetua's account**, **her father as reported**, and **the official as reported**. This is a change of documentary lens, not three recovered depositions. Preserve the account's sorrow for her father; do not flatten him into a villain or invent his thoughts.
3. **The child is not returned.** §6. The line connecting mother and child is visibly interrupted. Her request is refused; the account interprets relief from nursing as God's will. Let visitors inspect the distinction between the reported event and its theological interpretation. Do not invent the infant's later fate or suggest the visitor can reunite them.
4. **A vision, then a waking interpretation.** §10, with an optional return to §4. An outline opens around the source register; no photoreal heaven, Egyptian opponent or violent animation. The visitor reveals separately what the text reports seeing and what Perpetua says it means. Do not turn a reported vision into external footage, treat its gender imagery as a diagnosis, or silently collapse it into the final arena narrative.
5. **Felicity, through another voice.** §15. A second, distinctly shaped relation diagram: narrator → Felicity → newborn. The narrator reports childbirth and another Christian woman taking the daughter to rear. No synthetic diary, invented interiority or universal legal claim derived only from this paragraph. The visitor can compare the two mothers' documentary access. An explicit note distinguishes Saturus's separate vision sections, §§11–13, from this narrator.
6. **Someone else finishes.** End of §10; §§18–21. Attributed writing ends before the games; a narrator describes the deaths. State that outcome plainly without a graphic reenactment, triumphal sound or suspense that obscures it. An empty space where the source register previously marked Perpetua's writing becomes the composition's final motif. The question concerns whose voice survives, not whether the visitor would be brave enough.

### Art direction and interaction

An intimate theatre of relationships. Deep plum `#302335`, violet clay `#614b68`, pale lilac `#f0eaf3`, muted rose `#e7b6c5`, dark ink `#271f2d`. These colors are contemporary editorial choices. Playfair for scene language, Geist for documentary voice and controls. Broad negative space and a responsive original vector diagram carry the separation motif. No bars, cells, clothing or architecture masquerading as reconstruction. The abstract relation marks change instantly on keyboard input; optional pointer press feedback is under 160ms and removed under reduced motion.

A compact, persistent scene index uses the actual six-part authored order. The encounter occupies one stage; **Previous** and a named **Continue** link replace endless scrolling. Every scene is directly linkable. Back/forward restores the scene. No-JS renders the full, correctly ordered account with anchor navigation and native disclosures. A full-account option is always available. No timed progression, audio, countdown, achievement or forced completion.

The right edge is a source register: speaking position, exact section, original-summary label, and a native evidence disclosure. On mobile it follows the short scene text and precedes onward navigation. Relation choices remain buttons with visible selection and explicit accessible names. Reading order must not depend on the diagram.

### Wider-world connections

- **Atlas:** locate Carthage at city scale using the existing camera and Pleiades record; explicitly do not mark a verified prison or arena. Geography is an orientation layer, never corroboration of the narrative's details.
- **Witnesses:** compare Pliny's coercive interrogations in another time and province. Their pairing is an editorial comparison, not evidence that his procedure caused Perpetua's case.
- **Voices and manuscripts:** the source register makes the composite work a material of the experience. A received English edition is not an autograph. No manuscript image is implied when none has been rights-cleared.
- **Stories:** the Christian narrator's scriptural frame can be inspected in the complete Passion; the existing pinned Jonah remains a distinct Scripture route. Do not manufacture a direct literary dependence on Jonah.
- **Art and culture:** the original relationship drawing is a disclosed modern visual interpretation of textual access. A later artwork may be added only if a particular reception question and verified rights justify it. Silence currently serves the encounter better than an unlicensed prison soundscape.

## Augustine — The Garden

### Dramatic center and six questions

This is Augustine's remembered and theologically interpreted struggle in **Confessions VIII**, addressed to God. The encounter's tension is the distance between willing and acting. Visitors follow a literary structure, not a diagnosis or a reconstructed live recording of conversion.

| Question | Treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Whose story? | The retrospective narrator Augustine, with Alypius present in the garden and others' stories remembered within his narrative. |
| What survives? | A crafted confession to God. The remembered sound, the act of opening a book and the divine meaning Augustine assigns it are distinguishable layers. |
| Stakes in the work? | His account of divided will, habit, change and the inability to complete the act he says he desires. |
| What changes? | Stories heard become an immediate challenge; hesitation becomes reading; reading is narrated as transformation; Alypius reads further and Monica is told. |
| Interactive discovery? | Separate and reunite the book's narrative strands: remembered examples, present action, scriptural reading and retrospective interpretation. See how the apparently isolated garden moment contains other people's stories. |
| Open question? | What changes when an event is told as an answer to God rather than a self-contained account of private resolve? |

### Four scenes

1. **Other lives enter the room.** VIII.1–5: Simplicianus and Victorinus; VIII.6–7: Ponticianus, Antony and the imperial agents. A typographic constellation gathers the stories Augustine recounts hearing. Select a name to see exactly which narrated encounter places it here. These are not independent witnesses to the garden.
2. **To will, and not to will.** VIII.8–11. Two clauses occupy opposite sides of a shallow horizontal space. Bringing them together reveals Augustine's argument for a divided will, not two independent souls. The spatial device visualizes his argument, not clinical psychology. No drag-only control: paired buttons and ordinary text expose the same structure.
3. **Hearing / interpreting / reading.** VIII.12. Three sequential, reader-controlled cuts. First the remembered child's sound, whose singer Augustine cannot identify as boy or girl; then the significance he assigns it; then his reading of the apostolic passage. The sound is textual by default. A future performed reading would be labeled modern performance, with consent and transcript. Never synthesize an authentic child's voice.
4. **The reading continues.** VIII.12's ending: Alypius reads further, Monica hears the account. The page opens its lateral space to other people rather than ending with an isolated triumphant silhouette. Return to the literary opening: a confession addressed to God. Leave a question about narration and change.

### Visual storyboard and materials

Pearl `#eef0e8`, leaf grey `#b9c6b0`, deep green `#243b32`, plum `#5b4057`, ink `#26342d`. Predominantly light, spacious, quietly asymmetrical. Type is the event: intervals, alignment and line breaks change with the reader's cut. An original schematic fig-leaf silhouette may mark the reported tree without reconstructing the garden. No scenic background, parallax or continuous music.

The Atlas could locate Milan without pinpointing an unverified garden. Confessions IX.7's communal singing and XI's time/psalm metaphor are meaningful onward paths, clearly outside VIII. Teresa's Life 9.8–10 is a documented later reading of this book. A later painting of conversion must be inspected as reception, never as evidence of the scene. A manuscript layer would distinguish a surviving copy from Augustine's hand and identify its textual contents before using it.

Primary reading actually re-opened: [Pusey's Confessions, Book VIII](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3296/3296-h/3296-h.htm#link2H_4_0008). Chapter/paragraph anchors vary by edition; the treatment's traditional chapter references must be checked against the edition chosen before implementing exact on-page excerpts. No Augustine quotation is newly shipped by this treatment.

## The Witnesses — Rome Remembers

### Dramatic center and six questions

An investigative desk whose subject is the distance between an event and its later record. The title names a Roman frame; **Josephus remains a distinct Jewish author**, not a Roman voice absorbed into a uniform category. The visitor investigates what a document can support, rather than accumulating evidence toward a predetermined verdict.

| Question | Treatment |
| --- | --- |
| Whose story? | People and communities named through Tacitus, Pliny/Trajan, Josephus and Suetonius, each with different purposes and access. |
| What survives? | Transmitted literary texts, a letter/reply pair and contested wording; no newly discovered court archive or autograph. |
| Stakes in the records? | Accusation after fire in Tacitus, interrogation and policy in Pliny, authority and James's death in Josephus, ambiguous identification in Suetonius. |
| What changes? | A familiar name becomes several different evidentiary problems. A later reference to an earlier event ceases to look like eyewitness testimony. |
| Interactive discovery? | Align documents by subject date or composition period; compare two on a chosen question; expose shared transmission so multiple versions are not counted as independent witnesses. |
| Open question? | Which conclusion survives when the documents' dates, purposes and limitations remain visible together? |

### Four investigative scenes

1. **An event. A later account.** Tacitus Annals 15.44. Two independently labeled temporal bands distinguish the narrated fire under Nero from Tacitus's later writing. Click the interval to inspect the basis and uncertainty of each date. No one exact composition year until a source explicitly supports it. The name Christus cannot turn this into an eyewitness document.
2. **The official asks for instructions.** Pliny 10.96 and Trajan 10.97. A reply is physically adjacent to its question. Visitors reveal interrogation, coercion and policy separately. Those forced to speak under torture are not free witnesses on a neutral interview tape. Do not stage their unheard voices.
3. **Two passages, one author, different problems.** Josephus 20.200 and 18.63–64. A split sheet keeps the James account apart from the received Testimonium. Selecting **What is disputed?** marks the actual question about wording/transmission without claiming a universally agreed original. Whealey's Syriac/Arabic argument is one sourced position; public abstract access is explicit. Do not fabricate a consensus percentage.
4. **A name that resists a verdict.** Suetonius Claudius 25.4. Chrestus remains an unresolved identification. On the comparison desk, select a question such as **Does this author claim to have seen Jesus?** The response cites the actual passage's limits. Final state retains unresolved items instead of awarding a proof score.

### Visual storyboard and materials

Porcelain `#e6edf1`, ultramarine `#284c8c`, slate `#3b4b61`, muted raspberry `#a94667`, white `#ffffff`. A daylight documentary workbench with moveable **selection**, not simulated paper. A single selected pair dominates the viewport. One document on narrow screens, with explicit A/B switching and a persistent textual comparison summary. No tiny pinboard, string conspiracy motif, ornamental stamps or long card inventory.

The Atlas locates the distinct geographical settings and reveals that Rome, Bithynia and Judaea are not one place. Dates need ranges and source locators. Manuscript transmission appears as a sourced lineage diagram, not an invented handwriting facsimile. Historical art can illustrate reception of Nero or Rome only with its creation date adjacent. Pliny connects to Perpetua as a comparison across time; Scripture remains separately accessible and is not treated as one more hostile record.

Primary passages and existing scholarly limitations are already inspectable in the [28-source ledger](research/human-story-sources.md) and typed graph. Before building this treatment, research composition-date ranges and manuscript lineages specifically; the current graph does not establish a full textual stemma. A search result or repeated modern translation is not another independent ancient witness.

## Selected vertical slice and critique

Choose **Perpetua** first. The existing primary source provides a tightly bounded sequence and a meaningful interaction: visitors can distinguish a person's attributed words from a voice speaking about her, while the mother/child relationship changes within the same narrative. The risk is turning persecution into spectacle or making modern emotional identification dictate the text; relational abstraction and explicit voice attribution address that risk.

| Before | After | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Common reading-room template | Six reader-controlled scenes with a source register | The form follows this composite narrative's changing voice |
| All context visible at once | Relationship choices reveal specific source observations | Visitors can discover access, absence and mediation |
| A source list at the end | Exact section and speaker remain beside every scene | Narrative tension does not erase evidentiary boundaries |
| Scroll through repeated explanatory blocks | One short scene at a time; full account and direct links always available | Pace remains under reader control |

## Verification and comprehension protocol

Technical acceptance: keyboard-only scene and relationship selection; deep link/back/forward; no-JS full account; 320/390/820/1440px; 200% text; reduced motion; axe; no errors or core network dependency; existing Nineveh/Jonah/Atlas regressions. Loading must never hide source text. There are no external media requests or optional audio in this slice.

Comprehension assertions in browser tests verify that the interface actually exposes: who the account attributes writing to, why the child matters, who reports Felicity, how visions differ from narrated external events, and where attributed writing ends. These checks do **not** establish that a person understood or was moved.

Independent visitor study remains required. Give an unfamiliar reader only the entrance link; do not explain the intended distinction. Observe their first action and exits, then ask: Who were we encountering? What changed for the child? Whose account describes Felicity? Did you understand the vision as independently verified? How would you check one claim? What question remains? Record their words, mistaken inferences and source-use behavior without correcting them until the debrief. Use both desktop and phone, include keyboard/screen-reader users, and revise if attribution or stakes are missed. No participant responses have been collected or invented.
