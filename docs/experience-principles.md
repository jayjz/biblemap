# BibleMap experience principles

BibleMap explores biblical stories through Scripture, geography, historical art, theology, and human reflection. The creative thesis is: *Ancient stories. The human condition. Questions that stay with us.*

## Visitor progression

The intended emotional movement is not a mandatory interface sequence. It is the progression we want to support:

1. **Recognition** — Something in the visitor’s life feels familiar enough to notice.
2. **Curiosity** — Why does this ancient story matter? What happens next?
3. **Encounter** — The biblical story is allowed to speak on its own terms.
4. **Reflection** — The visitor leaves with a question of their own, not a predetermined conclusion.

## Recognition is invitation, not interpretation

Recognition is an opening, not a diagnosis, psychological reading, or confirmation of what the visitor already believes. It does not assign Jonah a motive, reduce him to a symbol of avoidance, or tell the visitor what the story means about them.

Scripture is permitted to complicate, contradict, or surprise the recognition that brought someone in. Jonah 4:2 supplies Jonah’s stated objection only after the reader has encountered the preceding chapters. Jonah 4:11 ends with an unresolved question. Neither should be collapsed into the opening.

**Rejected objective:** “Make every visitor recognize themselves in biblical stories.”

**Actual objective:** Create an opportunity for recognition, then let Scripture complicate what visitors think they recognize.

## Constraints for the opening

- The first viewport must not predetermine Jonah’s psychology or moral situation.
- Questions and invitations remain optional. All paths enter the same story at Jonah 1:1.
- Do not explain the project’s purpose before offering a concrete human situation that might be recognized.
- Preserve room for the sailors, Nineveh, divine mercy, Jonah’s anger, and the open ending.

## Evidence, not intention

Artistic intention and observed visitor response are different things. Passing technical checks, accessibility tests, or editorial review does not establish that a stranger experienced recognition or curiosity. Emotional claims require observation of people who have not been briefed on the principle and who have not recently read the story. Indifference and early exits are data.

These principles govern creative and technical decisions for the Jonah experience. They do not replace Scripture-first safeguards, source transparency, or human editorial authority.

## Perspectives earn their place

Creative inclusion is driven by meaningful contextual connection, not author fame, source prestige, or a requirement that a thinker directly commented on Jonah. A related argument about mercy, flight or responsibility can illuminate a passage, provided BibleMap identifies its own proposed connection and preserves the source's actual context and differences.

Primary works, established translations, reputable reprints, published interviews/lectures and credible secondary collections are legitimate research sources. Verify wording, attribution and an exact obtainable location before quoting; distinguish paraphrase from quotation. Modern translation and reproduction rights remain separate from the age of the original work. Human review governs final selection, interpretation, copy and placement, not whether a candidate may be explored.

Some scenes need silence. The biblical passage remains primary; a recurring template must not require a quotation, reflection question or historical image in every scene. See the maintained [Jonah completion blueprint](jonah-completion.md).
