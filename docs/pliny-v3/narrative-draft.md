# Pliny V3 · A kind of commonwealth

Complete first literary draft. AI-authored editorial experiment for human review; not publication approval. 15 November 2026. Written before the visual studies. Quotations below are William Melmoth, revised F. C. T. Bosanquet, Project Gutenberg 2811. Modern numbering is supplied; this edition calls 8.16 XC, 10.33–34 XLII–XLIII, and 10.96–97 XCVII–XCVIII. Source statements remain statements by their writers, not independently verified events.

There have been deaths in the household. Pliny writes to his friend Paternus about them. Some of those who died were young. He says that the losses have been a great affliction to him.

The people in this letter include people he enslaves. He has two consolations. He has readily granted freedom, so that some have lived long enough to die free. And he allows enslaved members of his household to make a kind of will. Their requests do not have the standing of a legal testament. He says he observes them as though they did.

> I receive and obey their last requests and injunctions as so many authoritative commands,
>
> — Pliny, Letters 8.16, excerpt

They may leave their possessions to whomever they choose, on one condition: the beneficiary must belong to his household. Pliny calls the house, for those enslaved within it, a kind of state and commonwealth.

He writes that these concessions do not make his grief manageable. He does not wish to become harder. Other people, he says, treat such deaths as a loss of property and call their indifference philosophy. He will not dispute their philosophy. He disputes their humanity.

These are words Pliny chooses to send to a friend. Another part of his correspondence takes us into Bithynia-Pontus, the Roman province he governs in the early second century. Here his correspondent is the emperor Trajan.

At Nicomedia, Pliny reports, a fire has destroyed private houses and public buildings. The city lacked the equipment to fight it. He has ordered equipment prepared. He asks permission to form a company of 150 firemen. Only men who genuinely do the work will be admitted, he promises; the company will be small enough to supervise.

Trajan refuses the association. Whatever its name or purpose, he writes, it will become a faction. Provide equipment instead. Require property owners to help. Call on the crowd if necessary.

This exchange does not explain every proceeding that follows in the collection. It puts one of the emperor's concerns on the page: what happens when people form a body of their own.

People have also been brought before Pliny as Christians. In his letter about them he asks what should be punished: offenses associated with the name, or the name itself without another offense. Should age make a difference? Can someone who has ceased to be Christian receive pardon?

He has already acted. He reports asking whether the accused are Christians, repeating the question, and threatening punishment. Those who persist are ordered away to execution. Melmoth's English says "punished" here; another inspected translation makes execution explicit. No method or number is supplied. Roman citizens are marked for transfer to Rome.

Pliny says that, whatever the nature of their opinions, he is certain their obstinacy deserves punishment.

An anonymous list introduces more names. Some people deny that they are or have been Christians. Following words he supplies, they invoke the gods. They offer wine and incense before the emperor's image, brought in for this purpose together with images of the gods. They curse Christ. He says he releases them.

Others say that they once belonged but have ceased to belong. They perform the same acts. The letter does not give us the convictions behind them.

From people he has questioned, Pliny relays an account of a fixed day and a gathering before daylight. They address Christ as divine. They bind themselves against wrongdoing: theft, adultery, breach of trust, refusing to return property entrusted to them. They disperse and meet again to eat ordinary food together.

They say they have stopped the latter gathering since his edict prohibiting associations, issued under the emperor's instructions. Even this account of a meal reaches us through an investigation.

Pliny then describes what he did to find out more.

He says he tortured two enslaved women who were called ministrae. The word suggests a ministering role; its exact duties are not explained. Neither woman speaks directly in this letter, and it does not tell us what happened to them afterward.

The earlier letter to Paternus is still available to read. In that letter, Pliny says he receives and obeys the last requests of members of his enslaved household as commands. Here he reports seeking information from two enslaved women through torture. These accounts belong to the same writer. Neither supplies his thoughts about the other.

He reports finding only what he calls a depraved and excessive superstition. He suspends the investigation and asks Trajan for instructions. He writes of people of every age and rank, of both sexes, in cities, villages and the countryside. Many are at risk, and more will be.

Then he describes a change. Temples that were almost deserted are being frequented. Sacred rites are returning. There is demand again for sacrificial offerings that had found few buyers. From this he conjectures how many people might be reclaimed if they are allowed to repent.

These are his observations and his account of improvement. The letter cannot independently establish how widespread the change was, or what caused every return.

Trajan approves the course he has taken. He declines to make a general rule for every case. Christians are not to be sought out. If brought forward and proved, they are to be punished. Anonymous accusations are not to be admitted.

There is a way to obtain pardon. Someone must deny being Christian and demonstrate the denial by worshipping the Roman gods. Suspicion in the past need not prevent release.

The emperor's answer leaves that act within reach: invoke our gods.

## Later reception, outside the ending

In Apology II.6–9, Tertullian later objects to the combination of no search and continued punishment. This is Christian advocacy interpreting the exchange, not testimony from the accused. It belongs after the complete reading or within a comparison, not as an authority that settles its meaning.

## First-draft critique before design

- The opening contains real circumstances but explains the legal concession too early. Let the exact sentence be encountered before its boundary is explained.
- The fire brigade material is useful context but consumes too much space before the community arrives. Compress it; do not make the letter collection into a chronology or one causal chain.
- The central paragraph explains the comparison after both passages have already made it. Remove its moral scaffolding; bring the two exact excerpts into contact and let their distinct contexts remain visible.
- The community needs its religious commitments and practical obligations together. Preserve Christ, trust, deposited property and the shared food; do not reduce it to either a harmless club or a group defined only by punishment.
- The ending is specific, but the final line risks sounding like a slogan. Finish on the complete condition of pardon, in the identified historical voice, without a modern concluding question.
