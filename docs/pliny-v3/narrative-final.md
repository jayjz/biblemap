There have been deaths in the household.

Pliny writes to his friend Paternus about them. Some of those who died were young. He says the losses have been a great affliction to him.

Pliny the Younger, Roman governor of Bithynia-Pontus in the early second century, is known here through his letters. This one concerns people in his own household.

They include people he enslaves. He has two consolations. He has readily granted freedom, so that some have lived long enough to die free. And he allows enslaved household members to make a kind of will. He says he honors their requests as though they had the standing of legal testaments.

> I receive and obey their last requests and injunctions as so many authoritative commands,
>
> — Pliny to Paternus, 8.16 · Melmoth / Bosanquet · excerpt

They may leave their possessions to whomever they choose, on one condition: the beneficiary must belong to his household.

> for to slaves the house they are in is a kind of state and commonwealth, so to speak.
>
> — Pliny to Paternus, 8.16 · Melmoth / Bosanquet · excerpt

These concessions do not settle his grief. He writes that he does not wish to become harder. Other people, he says, treat such deaths as a loss of property and call their indifference philosophy. He will not dispute their philosophy. He disputes their humanity.

Elsewhere in the collection, his correspondent is the emperor Trajan. Pliny is reporting on the province he governs.

At Nicomedia, a fire has destroyed private houses and public buildings. The city lacked equipment to fight it, Pliny reports. He has ordered equipment prepared. He asks to form a company of 150 firemen: genuine workers, a number small enough to supervise.

Trajan refuses the association. Provide equipment instead, he says. Require property owners to help. Call on the crowd if necessary.

> Whatever name we give them, and for whatever purposes they may be founded, they will not fail to form themselves into factious assemblies, however short their meetings may be.
>
> — Trajan to Pliny, 10.34 · Melmoth / Bosanquet · excerpt

This is one exchange about one proposed association. It does not supply a complete explanation of the proceedings in another letter.

People have been brought before Pliny as Christians. He asks Trajan whether the name alone should be punished without another offense. Should age make a difference? Can someone who has ceased to be Christian receive pardon?

He has already acted. He reports asking whether the accused are Christians, repeating the question, and threatening punishment. Those who persist are ordered away to execution. Roman citizens are marked for transfer to Rome.

Whatever the nature of their opinions, he writes, their obstinacy certainly deserves punishment.

An anonymous list introduces more names. Some people deny that they are or have been Christians. Following words he supplies, they invoke the gods. They offer wine and incense before the emperor's image, brought in for this purpose with images of the gods. They curse Christ. He says he releases them.

Others say that they once belonged but have ceased to belong. They perform the same acts. The letter does not give us the convictions behind them.

From people he has questioned, Pliny relays an account of a fixed day and a gathering before daylight. They address Christ as divine. They bind themselves against wrongdoing: fraud, theft, adultery, breaking their word, refusing to return property entrusted to them. They disperse and meet again for food.

> to eat in common a harmless meal.
>
> — Pliny, reporting the account given to him, 10.96.7 · Melmoth / Bosanquet · excerpt

They say they have stopped the latter gathering since his edict against assemblies, issued under the emperor's instructions. Their account of food, worship and obligations reaches us through his investigation.

Pliny writes that receiving this account made further inquiry more necessary. He describes seeking the truth by

> putting two female slaves to the torture
>
> — Pliny to Trajan, 10.96.8 · Melmoth / Bosanquet · excerpt

> I receive and obey their last requests and injunctions as so many authoritative commands,
>
> — Pliny to Paternus, 8.16 · Melmoth / Bosanquet · excerpt

The two enslaved women were called ministrae. The word indicates a ministering role; the letter does not explain their duties. Neither woman speaks directly here. Their subsequent fate is not given.

He reports finding only what he calls an absurd and extravagant superstition. He suspends the proceedings and asks Trajan for instructions.

He writes of people of every age and rank, of both sexes, in cities, villages and the countryside. Many are at risk, and more will be.

> The temples, at least, which were once almost deserted, begin now to be frequented;
>
> — Pliny to Trajan, 10.96.10 · Melmoth / Bosanquet · excerpt

Sacred rites are being renewed. Demand has returned for sacrificial offerings that had found few buyers. From this he conjectures how many people might be reclaimed if they are allowed to repent.

This is Pliny's account of a change in the province. His letter does not independently establish its extent, or the reasons for every return.

Trajan approves the course he has taken. He declines to make a general rule for every case. Christians are not to be sought out. If brought forward and proved, they are to be punished. Anonymous accusations are not to be admitted.

To obtain pardon, someone must deny being Christian and demonstrate the denial through worship of the Roman gods. Past suspicion need not prevent release. Trajan gives the condition:

> by invoking our gods, let him (notwithstanding any former suspicion) be pardoned upon his repentance.
>
> — Trajan to Pliny, 10.97 · Melmoth / Bosanquet · excerpt
